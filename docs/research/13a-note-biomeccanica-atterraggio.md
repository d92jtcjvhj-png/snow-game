## Q1. Touchdown sequence, leg absorption, impact forces, maximum absorbable EFH, landing steepness

### Takeaway
Real snowboard landings mostly touch down **tail first** (57%), and tail-first is associated with lower impact loads. Measured peak board-to-binding compression is about **4 BW**, with very large spread (SD about 1.6 BW). It grows in both magnitude and rate with jump size. Lab drop landings show that a **soft landing** (about 55° of knee flexion range) cuts peak force from about 2.6 to about 1.6 BW compared with a stiff landing (about 37°).

**Correction to the project's "1.5 m" figure:** the Minetti leg-muscle shock-absorber paper is *Ergonomics* **1998**, not 2010. Its model gives a safe drop-landing limit of about **1.6–2.0 m for sedentary people and 2.6–3.0 m for ski-team athletes**. The "1.5 m" design value is a conservative design number quoted by Petrone/McNeil/Hubbard. It is not a hard physiological ceiling for elite riders.

### Cited Findings
**Touchdown order and pitch**
- Field observation: 57% of ground contacts were tail-first, 32% flat and 11% nose-first. An earlier kinetic study by McAlpine found lower impact loads with tail-first landings — [BISp record of McAlpine/Kurpiers/Kersting, "Jump landings in snowboarding: an observational study"](https://www.bisp-surf.de/Record/PU201111009632); [SPONET](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/3042548?lng=en)

**Impact forces measured on the board**
- Custom snowboard force plates recorded all three GRF components at 1000 Hz. The forces and the joint kinematics/kinetics were sensitive to boot wear, binding angle and jump dimensions. "Both magnitude and rate of measured GRFs [increase] with jumps of greater size" — [McAlpine PhD thesis record, Univ. Auckland, "Biomechanical Analysis of Snowboard Jump Landings: A Focus on the Ankle Joint Complex"](https://researchspace.auckland.ac.nz/handle/2292/6655?show=full); [ISBS 2012 "Biomechanical analyses of snowboard jump landings"](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/5172) (snippet)
- Population mean peak **compression force was 4.14 BW (SD 1.63) at the front foot and 4.10 BW (SD 1.66) at the back foot**. "During landing the snowboarder is forced into flexion at the ankle and knee." This is a snippet. It is unclear whether the BW figure is per foot or per plate — [ISBS 2012, McAlpine et al.](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/5172/4748) (snippet)
- In a 2006 pilot, the impact-phase vertical GRF peaks were **4.79 BW (SD 0.66) and 3.74 BW (SD 0.30)** for the two participants — [ISBS pilot, "Development of a field testing protocol for the biomechanical analysis of snowboard jump landings"](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/232) (snippet)
- German national halfpipe team (n = 6, 500 Hz plates between board and binding). Landing forces were "generally more evenly distributed" between the feet than at take-off. At take-off the rear/front split was about 65/35 on straight airs and about 55/45 switch — [SPONET 4075893](https://www.sponet.de/sponet/Record/4075893?lng=en)
- Elite freestyle snowboarders (LJMU MPhil, Noonan 2018) were measured with board acceleration (g), knee flexion and knee angular velocity on regular, switch and 360 landings. **Regular straight-air landings showed the largest peak accelerations and were landed with less knee flexion.** The study recommends hamstring/quadriceps strength and rate of force development. The g values were not retrieved — [LJMU eprint 9585](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/9585/); [slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/the-biomechanical-demands-of-elite-freestyle-snowboard-athletes-mphil-presentation/129210321)

**Comparison sports**
- Ski slopestyle (pressure insoles): big-air landing forces were 1446 ± 367 N (**2.04 ± 0.46 × body mass**) for a 180 and 1409 ± 257 N (1.99 ± 0.28) for a switch 180. There was no difference between the two — [Löfquist et al., PMC7745909](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7745909) (snippet). Note that insoles typically under-read peak forces compared with plates. That is my caveat, not the source's.
- Ski jumping (22 juniors, force insoles): GRFmax was **1.1–5.3 BW per foot**, with both telemark and parallel landings. Earlier studies reported more than 4 BW — [Bessone, Petrat, Schwirtz, *Sensors* 2019, PMC6539877](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6539877); [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/19/9/2011/html)

**Absorption technique (lab drop landings)**
- Drop landings: peak vGRF was **2.60 ± 1.32 BW stiff vs 1.63 ± 0.73 BW soft**. Total knee flexion ROM was **36.8° ± 11.1° stiff vs 55.4° ± 8.8° soft**. Stiff = more extended knee at contact. The classic DeVita & Skelly definition is "soft = knee flexion > 90° after a 59 cm drop" — [PubMed 21602566](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21602566) and related PMC articles in the same search ([PMC4167636](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4167636)) (snippets; the exact study attribution of each number is uncertain)
- Knee compression force was 11.8 / 17.0 / 20.2 N/kg for soft / natural / stiff instructions — [same search set, PMC9465758](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9465758) (snippet; attribution uncertain)

**Maximum absorbable impact (Minetti)**
- Minetti, Ardigò, Susta, Cotelli, "Using leg muscles as shock absorbers: theoretical predictions and experimental results of drop landing performance", *Ergonomics* **1998**. The model uses knee-extensor mechanics, limb geometry and maximal activation. Experiments used drops of 0.4 / 0.7 / 1.1 m with sedentary men and Italian alpine-ski team athletes. **Predicted safe landing limit: about 1.6–2.0 m for sedentary subjects and 2.6–3.0 m for athletes** — [BibBase](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/minetti-ardig-susta-cotelli-usinglegmusclesasshockabsorberstheoreticalpredictionsandexperimentalresultsofdroplandingperformance-1998); [AIR Unimi](https://air.unimi.it/handle/2434/19726) (snippets)
- 2010 Science & Skiing follow-up: 7 Italian downhill A-team skiers dropped from 0.8 m onto a force plate with "hard" vs "soft" instructions. Maximum negative power was 33.3 ± 8.1 W/kg, 62% higher than in 1998 — [SPONET 3047810](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/3047810?lng=en)
- Petrone et al. cite Minetti for "maximum normal component that a rider's legs can absorb is only 1.5 m" — [arXiv 1611.04448](https://pdf.arxiv.org/pdf/1611.04448) (snippet). This conflicts with the 1998 abstract's 1.6–3.0 m range. Most likely 1.5 m is a conservative design value with margin for recreational riders.

**Landing steepness and normal velocity**
- A triple-cork example carried about 16 m of total landing energy in EFH units, of which only 1.2 m was normal and 14.8 m tangential. A steep landing aligned with the trajectory converts almost everything into tangential speed — [arXiv 1611.04448](https://pdf.arxiv.org/pdf/1611.04448) (snippet)
- The constant-EFH jump was measured with an accelerometer-instrumented snowboard. EFH was estimated from the board-normal accelerometer axis and roughly agreed with theory. The 2017 version added jumper-mounted sensors — [ASTM STP 2015](https://store.astm.org/stp158220140027.html); [Springer 2017](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s12283-017-0253-y). The measured g values were not retrieved.

### Inferences
- **[DERIVED] Absorption time and leg stroke.** With constant deceleration, a normal velocity v_n absorbed over a leg/board stroke d gives a = v_n²/(2d) and t = 2d/v_n.
  - EFH 1.5 m means v_n = 5.4 m/s. With d ≈ 0.4 m (hip drop from about 25° to about 90° of knee flexion), a ≈ 3.7 g net, i.e. about **4.7 BW including gravity**, and t ≈ 0.15 s. This matches the measured about 4 BW.
  - EFH 0.5 m means v_n = 3.1 m/s. With d = 0.4 m, a ≈ 1.25 g (about 2.2 BW) and t ≈ 0.26 s.
  - A real force curve peaks early, at about 30–80 ms in drop studies (not retrieved, see Gaps). So peak force ≈ 1.5–2× the mean.
  - Game model: leg spring-damper with a stroke of about 0.35–0.45 m and compression completed in about **0.15–0.3 s**. This fits the 0.5 s window.
- **[DERIVED] Max EFH versus leg stroke.** With available stroke d and tolerable mean force F/BW, the limit is EFH_max ≈ d·(F/BW − 1). For d = 0.45 m and F = 6–7 BW, that gives about 2.3–2.7 m, consistent with Minetti's 2.6–3.0 m for athletes. A stiff, extended rider with d = 0.2 m reaches only about 1 m. So the absorbable EFH should scale with how much flexion is left at touchdown. A rider landing already squatted (a grab still held) has less stroke.
- **[DERIVED] Steeper landing is softer.** v_n = |v|·sin(θ_traj − θ_slope), so every degree of mismatch matters more at high speed.
- **[ESTIMATE] Tail-first.** Model a slight nose-up pitch at touchdown, about 0–10° relative to the slope, as the default "clean" pitch. The tail contacts first and the pitch rotates down over about 50–100 ms, spreading the impact. Nose-first is the bad case: OR about 4 for falls (previous notes).

### Gaps
- No retrieved time-to-peak force for snowboard landings. The drop-landing time-to-peak was also not retrieved, so the 30–80 ms above is my recollection, not cited.
- No numeric knee/hip flexion angles at touchdown or at peak for snowboard landings (in the McAlpine and Noonan theses, but not readable).
- The Noonan peak-g values and the Petrone measured g values were not retrieved.
- It is unclear whether McAlpine's 4.1 BW figure is per foot.

## Q2. Yaw misalignment at touchdown: sideslip, edge catch, washout

### Takeaway
No source quantifies a ridable yaw angle. Mechanically, a board yawed against travel is skidding. If the skid is carried by the **uphill** edge (the trailing edge relative to the sideways velocity), the board is a brake and the rider can ride it out. If the **downhill/leading** edge engages, it bites and stops the board almost instantly while the body's momentum continues. That moment pitches the rider over the leading edge: a "catch", usually a backward heel-side slam or a forward toe-side one. Coaching warns that open landings often end in toe-edge catches, and that edge-angle changes during touchdown invite washouts.

### Cited Findings
- "The downhill edge is the one that can catch". It "bites into the snow and abruptly halts the board", throwing the rider down. Grip and braking resistance grow with edge angle. A board pointing across the hill while momentum continues downhill is the classic set-up — [Science Insights](https://scienceinsights.org/what-is-catching-an-edge-in-snowboarding/) (enthusiast source)
- A medical review describes an edge catch as the edge facing the mountain "suddenly and unintentionally catches on the snow surface", typically during downhill runs — [Thieme review, s-0041-1731702](https://thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/html/10.1055/s-0041-1731702) (snippet)
- Backward falls often start when an edge catches, "sometimes on the opposite edge" — [UBC FIB book wiki, wrist injuries in snowboarding falls](https://wiki.ubc.ca/Documentation:FIB_book/Wrist_Injuries_and_Injury_Prevention_Methods_in_Snowboarding_Falls) (secondary)
- Spin landings: backside spins take off on the toe edge and "land blind catching the landing with your heel edge". Open landings can end in a toe-edge catch, "the usual cause of a bad landing" — [Snowboard Addiction, how to land spins](https://snowboardaddiction.com/blogs/training-education/how-to-land-spins) (snippet)
- Lateral friction depends on edge angle, and edge pressure from the front foot first reduces catches. These are coaching sources — [themanual](https://themanual.com/outdoors/snowboarding-tips-to-change-edges); [NC State Skiing & Snowboarding text, turning and speed control](https://ncstate.pressbooks.pub/skiingandsnowboarding/chapter/turning/)

### Inferences
- **[DERIVED] Edge-catch mechanics for the game.**
  - Let v_lat be the sideways velocity of the board relative to snow, and let the board roll angle be φ_edge.
  - If the edge on the side **toward** v_lat (the leading edge) is lower than the base plane, snow shear on that edge creates a large retarding force F at ground level. The rider's CoM sits h ≈ 0.9–1.0 m above the board.
  - The torque F·h about the board tips the CoM over the leading edge.
  - Example: an abrupt stop of the lateral component v_lat = v·sin(yaw). At v = 15 m/s and yaw = 20°, v_lat = 5.1 m/s. Stopped in about 0.1 s, that is about 5 g lateral at the feet, which no rider can balance. That is a guaranteed fall.
  - With the **trailing** (uphill) edge loaded, the lateral force pushes the feet back under the CoM, which is stable. The project's anisotropic friction then dissipates v_lat as a skid.
- **[ESTIMATE] Rule.**
  - Flat base or trailing edge engaged: skid, with speed loss from lateral friction. Clean if yaw is under about 10–15°, sketchy at about 15–35°.
  - Leading edge engaged (edge angle above a few degrees toward v_lat): catch, then a fall in the direction of v_lat. The heel-side case is a back slam (heel/butt), the toe-side case a face/knees fall.
- **[DERIVED] Heel washout.** If the heel edge carries a too-large edge angle and too little load (rider leaning back, weight on the tail), the edge loses grip, the tail slides out and the rider sits down. This is the "washout" of coaching jargon. Game rule: heel-edge touchdown with CoM behind the board means a washout/butt-check, not an edge catch.

### Gaps
- No quantitative yaw tolerance found (same as the previous notes).
- No measured edge-catch decelerations.

## Q3. Residual rotation at touchdown, under- vs over-rotation, switch/revert

### Takeaway
Coaching treats **over-rotation → revert** as the most common spin error. A **blind landing** (slightly counter-rotated upper body, gripping with the edge) is the standard way to stop residual spin. Coaches explicitly prefer a slight under-rotation over an over-rotation on backside 180s. No source gives survivable residual spin rates in °/s.

### Cited Findings
- Over-rotation is often caused by too much initial spin force or by trying to spot the landing mid-spin, which leads to "over-rotating their trick and reverting out" — [Snowboard Addiction, backside 360 live coaching](https://snowboardaddiction.com/blogs/training-education/why-your-backside-360-isn-t-working-live-coaching); [SA, frontside 360 problems](https://snowboardaddiction.com/blogs/spins/16098668-top-3-problems-when-learning-how-to-frontside-360-on-a-snowboard) (snippets)
- "A blind landing helps you stop your spin rotation and prevents you from reverting". A blind landing has the body slightly counter-rotated and grips the snow with the edge. Riders decide before the spin: "Is this an OPEN LANDING or a BLIND LANDING?" — [SA, how to land spins](https://snowboardaddiction.com/blogs/jumping/how-to-land-spins); [SA, clean cab 360s](https://snowboardaddiction.com/blogs/training-education/how-to-do-clean-cab-360s-on-park-jumps) (snippets)
- "It's better to slightly under-rotate a backside 180 than over-rotate" — [Maverix Snow, frontside/backside 180 keys (Medium)](https://medium.com/@maverixsnow/the-keys-to-landing-frontside-180-s-on-a-snowboard-1ad6fdcf8ed9) (snippet; the source is a coaching blog)
- Counter-rotation means the upper and lower body rotate in opposite directions — [SA rotation vs counter-rotation](https://snowboardaddiction.com/blogs/training-education/rotation-vs-counter-rotation)

### Inferences
- **[DERIVED] Stopping residual yaw spin with friction.**
  - A board spinning at ω about the slope normal while sliding experiences lateral friction torque. Rough estimate: τ ≈ μ_lat·N·L/4 for a uniformly loaded board of length L about its centre.
  - With μ_lat ≈ 0.5 (edge bite), N ≈ 3 BW at impact, m = 75 kg and L = 1.55 m: τ ≈ 0.5·2200·0.39 ≈ 430 N·m.
  - Rider plus board yaw inertia about the vertical axis is I ≈ 1.5–3 kg·m² (standing, arms in).
  - Angular deceleration is about 150–280 rad/s², so 360°/s (6.3 rad/s) stops in about 25–40 ms, turning only about 5–8°.
  - So friction **can** stop considerable spin during the impact window. The danger is not the stop itself but that the torque comes from **edge bite**, which also creates the catch condition of Q2.
  - Residual spin therefore turns into yaw error plus an edge load. Game rule: under-rotation leaves yaw error (skid). Over-rotation leaves yaw error **in the direction of continued rotation**, often with the leading edge biting (catch) or the board continuing round into a revert.
- **[ESTIMATE] Asymmetry.** Under-rotation is safer for backside 180s per coaching, because the rider can see the landing (blind side issue aside) and the board's residual spin direction is opposite to the yaw error, so friction corrects it. Over-rotation's residual spin increases the error. Model the revert (extra +180° turned at low speed loss) as a sketchy-but-ridden outcome, as in FIS judging (previous notes).
- **[ESTIMATE] Bands.** Survivable residual spin rate: below about 90°/s clean, 90–270°/s sketchy (skid/revert), above about 270°/s with yaw error over 30° a fall. Unsourced; to be tuned.

### Gaps
- No measured residual spin rates or yaw-inertia values for riders at landing.

## Q4. Body tilt/axis, pitch errors, lean back vs forward, flips

### Takeaway
Falls after jumps are mostly **backward/heel-side** (51% posterior in field data, from the previous notes). Backward falls produce about **twice as many wrist fractures** as forward falls, and higher hand-impact forces. Lunate/perilunate injuries are typical after a backward fall following an aerial. Backward-rotated or inverted landing configurations matter more than EFH for cervical injury (previous notes). No source gives numeric pitch tolerances.

### Cited Findings
- Backward falls cause about 2× more wrist fractures than forward falls. The mechanism is FOOSH (fall on outstretched hand) with a hyperextended, heavily loaded wrist — [UBC FIB book wiki: Kinetics of arm injuries in snowboarding falls](https://wiki.ubc.ca/Documentation:FIB_book/Kinetics_of_Arm_Injuries_in_Snowboarding_Falls); [Taylor & Francis chapter](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/e/9781315163291/chapters/10.4324/9781315163291-11) (secondary)
- Instrumented-glove study: 128 hand-impact falls from 20 snowboarders. Backward falls produced significantly higher forces than forward falls, and beginners took more load than advanced riders — ["Wrist biomechanics during snowboard falls", Proc IMechE P, 2013](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1754337113482706) (abstract only)
- Lunate/perilunate fractures and dislocations typically follow a backward fall after an aerial manoeuvre. Video shows palms striking the ground in front of the board — [UBC FIB book wiki, wrist injury prevention](https://wiki.ubc.ca/Documentation:FIB_book/Wrist_Injuries_and_Injury_Prevention_Methods_in_Snowboarding_Falls) (secondary)

### Inferences
- **[DERIVED] Pitch at touchdown.** Let Δθ be the board pitch relative to the slope, with nose-up positive.
  - Tail-first with a small Δθ (0–10°): the tail acts as a lever and the board slaps flat. The rider must absorb the pitch-down rate, and CoM placement over the feet decides the outcome.
  - With large nose-up (about 20°+, e.g. an under-rotated backflip): the tail digs in and the CoM is behind the tail contact. The rider continues rotating backward onto heels/butt/back, i.e. a butt-check or back slam. This matches the posterior fall dominance.
  - Nose-down (an over-rotated backflip, or a frontflip): the nose digs. Friction at the nose plus the forward CoM gives a forward pitch-over. That is a face plant, the OR ≈ 4 nose-first fall.
- **[ESTIMATE] Bands.** |Δθ| up to about 15° clean if the CoM is over the board, 15–30° sketchy (hand drag / butt check), above about 30° a fall. Roll (off-axis tilt about the travel direction) behaves like an edge angle at touchdown. If it puts the leading edge down, apply the Q2 catch rule.

### Gaps
- No quantitative pitch/roll tolerances for flips or corks found.

## Q5. Landing position: knuckle, table, outside the groomed landing, fences

### Takeaway
This is covered in the previous notes (knuckle 35% / table 20% of touchdowns, knuckle inversion, and flat/knuckle as significant fall predictors). The new material concerns soft snow only. Deep or soft snow resistance grows with penetration depth, but no study measures snowboard deceleration in powder.

### Cited Findings
- Penetration force scales with the volume of snow displaced. Shear force scales with penetration depth × tool length — [Journal of Glaciology, "Measurement of mechanical properties of snow for simulation of skiing"](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-glaciology/article/measurement-of-mechanical-properties-of-snow-for-simulation-of-skiing/00DD7880EF30BFB651D61146B3EFC3FA) (snippet)
- Penetration resistance pressure for homogeneous snow increases linearly with depth (inclined plate on groomed slopes) — [Cold Regions Sci. Tech., via same search](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-glaciology/article/measurement-of-mechanical-properties-of-snow-for-simulation-of-skiing/00DD7880EF30BFB651D61146B3EFC3FA) (snippet; exact paper not identified)
- Measured ski/snowboard friction coefficients range about 0.02–0.14. Wet snow at speed adds capillary drag — [KIT publication 1000172164](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000172164/153587108) (snippet)
- Planing/lift model: trapped air supplies about 50% of lift for a snowboard at 20 m/s on wind-packed snow. Penetration depth and angle of attack depend on snow type — [APS DFD 2005, "Lift mechanics of downhill skiing and snowboarding"](https://meetings-archive.aps.org/dfd/2005/am/11)

### Inferences
- **[DERIVED/ESTIMATE] Soft-snow model.** Normal resistance p = k·z, with z the sink depth, gives a sink equilibrium z = N/(k·A). The along-board drag (ploughing) is about p·(z·w), proportional to z².
  - A board landing with high normal velocity in soft snow absorbs part of the EFH by sinking, which makes it softer.
  - But the board decelerates strongly and pitches forward if the nose sinks, a nose-dive.
  - Game: off-piste zone with μ_eff of about 0.15–0.3 plus a sink-dependent drag, versus 0.06 on groomed snow (the legacy value). That gives deceleration of about 1.5–3 m/s² versus 0.6 m/s². A nose-down pitch there means a fall. Unsourced magnitudes.
- Fences: a Rapier collision is a crash (as in the previous notes).

### Gaps
- No measured snowboard deceleration or drag in powder or soft snow, and no measured friction for groomed versus soft snow after landing.

## Q6. Speed/drag after landing, groomed vs soft

### Takeaway
On groomed snow, after-landing deceleration comes from base friction (μ about 0.02–0.14, project value 0.06), aerodynamic drag, and any skid from yaw. The impact itself removes only the normal velocity component. Tangential speed (most of the energy, e.g. 14.8 of 16 m EFH-equivalent) is retained if the rider lands flat and aligned.

### Cited Findings
- Tangential vs normal energy split: 14.8 m vs 1.2 m on a triple-cork landing — [arXiv 1611.04448](https://pdf.arxiv.org/pdf/1611.04448) (snippet)
- Friction coefficient range 0.02–0.14 — [KIT](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000172164/153587108) (snippet)

### Inferences
- **[DERIVED]** Speed loss at touchdown ≈ the normal component only, for a frictionless impulse. With friction during impact there is an extra tangential impulse of about μ·∫N dt = μ·m·v_n. At μ = 0.06 and v_n = 5 m/s that is only about 0.3 m/s. With a skid (μ_lat about 0.4–0.6 × sin(yaw)), a 20° yaw costs about 0.7–1 m/s during impact, plus the ongoing skid. So yaw, not impact, is the main speed killer in a "sketchy" landing.

### Gaps
- No field measurements of speed before and after landing.
