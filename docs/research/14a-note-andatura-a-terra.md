# Ground riding posture of a freestyle snowboarder (straight approach, heel/toe carve, speed check, pre-jump)

Method note: in this session WebFetch was blocked for every host tried (ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de, ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, mdpi.com, semanticscholar, scitepress, snowboardaddiction, psia-nw). ## 1. Neutral / athletic stance on a straight approach

### Takeaway
Measured snowboard data exists only for halfpipe run-ups. There, back-knee flexion spans about 29° to 64° (5–13 m/s). Instructor sources all describe the stance the same way: ankles and knees flexed, upright trunk with no bending at the waist, head up looking ahead, arms relaxed, weight centred over the board. A good straight-glide key pose is about 30–35° knee flexion, about 15–20° ankle dorsiflexion, about 20–30° hip flexion and a trunk only slightly forward (**ESTIMATE**, built from the ranges below).

### Cited Findings
- In halfpipe run-ups, wearable sensors gave back-leg knee flexion of about 29±2° to 64±4° at speeds of about 5–13 m/s (ISBS 2024 pilot, n=1 former European Cup rider, GNSS+IMU+insoles) — [Thelen, Merz et al., ISBS 2024](https://commons.nmu.edu/isbs/vol42/iss1/87/); [ResearchGate PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382452736_IN-FIELD_BIOMECHANICS_OF_HALFPIPE_SNOWBOARDING_A_PILOT_STUDY). Only a snippet was seen. The range covers the whole run-up (including pumping in the transition), not a steady glide.
- One snowboard motion study reported that "the optimum angle for knee flexion was deemed to be 120°". This is almost certainly the included angle (180° = straight leg), which equals about 60° of flexion. The same study found that skilled riders used more trunk and pelvis rotation than unskilled ones — the study is cited in the search summary next to [Kinematical Analysis of the Turn Transition Phase of the Snowboarders' Carving Turn](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/273669279_Kinematical_Analysis_of_the_Turn_Transition_Phase_of_the_Snowboarders'_Carving_Turn). The attribution was not verified.
- Alpine skiing reference (not snowboarding): knee flexion during skiing typically ranges between 30° and 80° — [JYU thesis](https://jyx.jyu.fi/bitstream/123456789/81576/1/URN%3aNBN%3afi%3ajyu-202206093189.pdf).
- PSIA snowboard cue sheet: "ankles and knees flexed slightly to allow for maximum range of motion" — [PSIA-NW Snowboard Visual Cues](https://www.psia-nw.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/SnowboardVisualCues.pdf).
- Bending at the waist is called a fault because it "moves a ton of your weight over the snow". Riders lower themselves by bending the knees and stay upright, "not by bending over" — [UC Balance is Key](https://homepages.uc.edu/~whiteii/FINAL/basics.html); [Special Olympics Dry Land Training](https://www.specialolympicswisconsin.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/Snowboarding-Dry-Land-Training.pdf).
- Knees track over the toes and the arms stay relaxed — [REI How to Snowboard](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/how-to-snowboard).
- The head stays up. Looking down hurts balance — [NC State Skiing & Snowboarding](https://ncstate.pressbooks.pub/skiingandsnowboarding/?p=109); [UC Balance is Key](https://homepages.uc.edu/~whiteii/FINAL/basics.html).
- Sources disagree on the arms. Some say hands "out over the nose and tail of the board", others say "comfortably by your sides" — (search summary of [Snowboard Addiction balanced position](https://snowboardaddiction.com/blogs/riding/balanced-position-on-a-snowboard) and [Husky Winter Sports Athletic Stance](https://huskywintersports.github.io/fundamentals/Athletic-Stance)). Swinging the arms or upper body is treated as an inefficient flaw — [REI](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/how-to-snowboard).
- Weight spread evenly between the front and back foot on the approach — [Ridestore: How to jump on a snowboard](https://www.ridestore.com/mag/how-to-jump-on-a-snowboard/).
- Binding highback forward lean (which sets the minimum ankle dorsiflexion): Burton measures 90° as neutral and suggests 75–85° for groomers (that is, 5–15° of forward lean). Park riders usually run less lean for a more upright, mobile stance — [Burton bindings guide](https://www.burton.com/discover/s/article/how-to-adjust-snowboard-bindings); [SnowboardingDays](https://www.snowboardingdays.com/?p=27962). FLUX offers 0–12° of adjustment from its own neutral — [FLUX](https://flux-bindings.com/en/blogs/feature/15).

### Inferences
- **ESTIMATE, straight-glide key pose (both legs, mirrored by the duck angles):**
  - ankle dorsiflexion 15–20° (highback lean of about 5–10° plus boot flex);
  - knee flexion 30–35° (the lower end of the halfpipe run-up range; flexion grows toward 60° when the rider compresses);
  - hip flexion 25–35°;
  - trunk forward of vertical, measured from the board-normal axis, 10–15°;
  - pelvis over the board centre, centre of mass between the bindings, 50/50 load (a slight 50–55 % front bias on a fast in-run is plausible but unsourced);
  - head level, gaze 10–20 m ahead down the fall line, chin rotated toward the nose (lead) over the lead shoulder;
  - shoulders roughly parallel to the board's long axis (open about 10–20° toward the nose in duck stance);
  - arms relaxed, elbows bent about 30–60°, hands at hip or waist height, slightly away from the body (about 15–25° shoulder abduction), lead hand a little forward over the nose side.
- A good animator rule: shins stay parallel to each other in side view and tilt forward with the ankle angle. Knee flexion is roughly 1.5–2× the ankle dorsiflexion (**ESTIMATE**).
- "Skilled = more trunk/pelvis rotation" suggests a subtle counter-rotation layer for good riders. A stiff, square torso looks beginner-like.

### Gaps
- No measured ankle, hip or trunk angles for steady straight gliding were found. The Klous 2014 and halfpipe IMU full texts may contain them; they were not fetched.
- No numeric arm, elbow or hand-height data for ground riding exists in the sources found.

## 2. Freestyle binding setup: duck angles, stance width, knee orientation

### Takeaway
Park and big-air riders use a symmetric or near-symmetric duck stance, typically +12/−12 to +15/−15 (sometimes a softer back foot, −3 to −6, or something like +15/−9). Width is about shoulder width or a little wider (about 53–58 cm for men). Duck angles turn the knees outward (external tibial rotation), so the knees point roughly over the toes of each binding rather than toward the board nose.

### Cited Findings
- Freestyle riders often use a symmetrical duck of about +15/−15 to ride switch easily — [Dick's Sporting Goods](https://www.dickssportinggoods.com/protips/sports-and-activities/winter-sports/improve-snowboarding-stance).
- +12°/−12° for switch and park riders — [Nidecker](https://nidecker-eu.gorgias.help/en-US/find-your-natural-stance-1648424).
- Back binding typically −3° to −6° "for a bit of extra stability". Freestyle riders need the stability of a wide stance — [Red Bull stance guide](https://redbull.com/us-en/snowboard-stance-setup-guide).
- Starting width is about shoulder width — [Nidecker](https://www.nidecker.com/en/content/468-what-is-the-right-snowboard-stance-for-you). For freestyle, slightly wider: 22–23 in (56–58 cm) for men and 20–21 in (51–53 cm) for women — [Rome Snowboards](https://eu.romesnowboards.com/blogs/news/how-to-choose-your-snowboard-stance).
- A common neutral recommendation is front about 15° with the back foot at 0° — [Dick's](https://www.dickssportinggoods.com/protips/sports-and-activities/winter-sports/improve-snowboarding-stance).
- ETH Zurich (ISBS 2012) tested 10 expert freestyle riders with 3 stance widths × 2 angle setups (forward and duck). Binding parameters affected tibial rotation on a flat landing from a 51 cm drop. A negative back-foot angle reduced internal tibial rotation. Ollie height did not differ significantly, although the authors suggest that a wide duck stance "might be beneficial" — [List, Bründler et al., ISBS 2012](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/5302); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/INFLUENCE-OF-STANCE-WIDTH-AND-BINDING-ANGLES-ON-AND-List-Br%C3%BCndler/8a66903e46a65e1f352aea94ba216b65bdaaddf4).
- The same group notes "no evidence-based recommendations" for stance width and binding angles — ([ISBS paper](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/5302/4874)).
- More pronounced external foot rotation gave greater external moments at the boot and changes in subtalar motion (landing simulation, 10 riders) — search summary of ISBS landing work ([ISBS 1075](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/1075/1141)). The text was truncated and could not be verified.
- Thesis: the preferred stance gave a significantly greater knee-flexion range and a lower minimum flexion angle than a 9° binding condition — [Auckland ResearchSpace](https://researchspace.auckland.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/f0920606-59d9-4a6e-80c1-775702d00aa7/content).

### Inferences
- **ESTIMATE, rig defaults:** front +15°, back −12° (or −15° for a fully symmetric park look). Stance width about 0.31–0.33 × rider height (a 1.75 m rider gives about 55–58 cm, roughly shoulder width + 10–15 %). Each knee's flexion axis rotates with its foot angle, so under flexion the knees splay into a mild "A/duck" shape seen from the front. The back knee drops toward the front knee only on toe-side moves.
- With duck stance the pelvis faces more "forward-sideways" (about 0–10° open toward the nose) than with a forward stance (**ESTIMATE**).

### Gaps
- The exact widths tested in the ISBS 2012 study and the tibial-rotation values (full text not fetched).
- No study found that expresses freestyle width as a fraction of height. The 0.31–0.33 × height figure is an ESTIMATE from the manufacturers' absolute ranges.

## 3. Heel-side vs toe-side carving: inclination, angulation, upper body, arms

### Takeaway
Riders reach edge angle with a mix of whole-body inclination and angulation at the ankles, knees and hips. Angulation lets edge angle exceed body lean "up to a limit". Heel-side looks like sitting into a low chair, with hips stacked over the heel edge, dorsiflexed ankles and a fairly upright trunk. Toe-side looks like kneeling into the hill: knees driven toward the snow, ankles pressed forward, hips pushed inside, often led by the front shoulder. Heel-side reaches lower edge angles than toe-side because of ankle and knee anatomy. No study found gives measured joint angles separately for heel and toe carves.

### Cited Findings
- Inclination (whole-body lean) and angulation (bending at hips and knees) are separate. Angulation adjusts edge angle relative to inclination "only to a limit" — [Michaud & Duncumb, Physics of a Snowboard Carved Turn (Bomber)](https://bomberonline.3dcartstores.com/assets/images/PDFs/Physics-of-a-Snowboard-Carved-Turn.pdf).
- Bending at the hips and knees and flexing the ankles brings the CoM into the turn while keeping the boots clear of the snow — [same source](https://bomberonline.3dcartstores.com/assets/images/PDFs/Physics-of-a-Snowboard-Carved-Turn.pdf); [nicholaswmin alpine carving article](https://nicholaswmin.github.io/alpine/articles/carving-steeps/article.html).
- Edge angles are managed by a combination of inclination and angulation (PSIA movement analysis) — [PSIA-NW Movement Analysis](https://www.psia-nw.org/wp-content/uploads/Movement-Analysis-Complete-snowboard-.pdf).
- Japanese wearable-sensor study of carving: hip internal/external rotation twists the lower limb, while hip flexion/extension plus ankle plantar/dorsiflexion generate the edging angle. Ankle and knee extension are linked to board edge angle — [Dynamic motion analysis of snowboard turns (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/271891413_Dynamic_motion_analysis_of_snowboard_turns_by_the_measurement_of_motion_and_reaction_force_from_snow_surface); [Motion analysis of carving turns by reaction force](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/309324200_A_Study_on_Motion_Analysis_of_Carving_Turns_in_Snowboarding_by_Measurement_of_Reaction_Force_from_Snow_Surface).
- Turn-transition kinematics: hip angle and board–hip and board–shoulder twist are lowest at a specific transition event (E3) — [Kinematical Analysis of the Turn Transition Phase](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/273669279_Kinematical_Analysis_of_the_Turn_Transition_Phase_of_the_Snowboarders'_Carving_Turn).
- Klous et al. 2014 (motion capture, carved snowboard turn vs ski turn): snowboard joint moments were consistently greater, and flexion/extension moments were larger than in skiing — [Klous, Müller, Schwameder 2014](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2014/340272). Angle tables were not retrieved.
- Heel-side: flex so the toes come toward the shins and bend the knees "as if sitting in a chair". Move the hips over the heel edge and sink them toward the highbacks — [NC State Turning](https://ncstate.pressbooks.pub/skiingandsnowboarding/chapter/turning/); [Snowboard Addiction carving](https://snowboardaddiction.com/blogs/riding/11018837-how-to-carve-on-a-snowboard); [The House](https://www.the-house.com/portal/how-to-turn-heelside-and-toeside-on-a-snowboard/).
- Drop the butt straight down rather than pushing it out, so the joints stay stacked over the board — [Boardworld forum (anecdotal)](https://boardworld.com.au/forums/viewreply/30542).
- At the turn apex, knees go "together and into the hill"; keep shoulders level while the board tilts — [coaching curriculum, Google Doc](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1RCi40yDpOm6Xik6NBmt88wAHLFVi4AJW20dK6iyiJOg/export?format=pdf).
- Shoulder alignment is contradictory. One source says shoulders and hips stay parallel to the board on heel-side. Another says shoulders parallel to the slope concentrate weight on the inside edge — [Snowboard Addiction carving](https://snowboardaddiction.com/blogs/riding/11018837-how-to-carve-on-a-snowboard) (search summary; contradiction noted inside the source).
- Heel/toe asymmetry: it is harder to tip the board high on heel-side, but heel-side gives more power transmission. Toe-side is often started with the front shoulder because rotating the hips over the toe edge is less natural — [CASI, Asymmetry in Snowboarding](https://casi-acms.com/casi-blog/asymmetry-in-snowboarding/); [Whitelines, Asymmetric Sidecuts](https://whitelines.com/snowboard-gear/before-you-buy/asymmetric-sidecuts-explained.html).
- Rider board flex: the board front flexes more on heel-side turns than on toe-side ones (patent) — [US patent 5871224](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5871224).
- A ski (not snowboard) reference on inside/outside leg: the inside leg has higher hip and knee flexion and carries less load (0.50 vs 0.76 BW vertical knee force, Klous 2012) — cited in [ResearchGate: Klous carved vs skidded](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/235370559_Three-Dimensional_Knee_Joint_Loading_in_Alpine_Skiing_A_Comparison_Between_a_Carved_and_a_Skidded_Turn).
- High edge angles reach about 70° on groomed snow in one game design (a simulator design choice, not measured data) — [SnowboardAnywhere GitHub issue](https://github.com/drewkeriazes/SnowboardAnywhere/issues/273).

### Inferences
- Body lean in a steady carve follows the force balance: tan(φ) = v²/(g·R) (standard physics, not taken from these sources). The game can compute the inclination of the whole-body CoM–contact line from the physics and let angulation make up the gap to the physics edge angle.
- **ESTIMATE, heel-side carve key pose** (edge angle about 30–45° for a park rider's approach carve):
  - CoM inclination about 0.7–0.8 × edge angle, the rest from angulation;
  - knees 50–70° flexion, the back knee about 5–10° more than the front;
  - ankles dorsiflexed about 20–25° against the highbacks;
  - hips 50–70° flexion, butt dropping over the heel edge without pushing out behind;
  - trunk fairly upright (trunk-to-thigh angle opens), chest leaning slightly toward the toes to counterbalance;
  - shoulders roughly level with the slope (counter-tilted against the board) and opened slightly toward the nose;
  - lead arm forward over the nose at about chest height, rear arm low and slightly behind over the tail;
  - head turned toward the direction of travel, gaze along the turn exit.
- **ESTIMATE, toe-side carve key pose:**
  - can reach a higher edge angle (about 40–55°), with more pure inclination;
  - knees 40–60° flexion, driven forward and down toward the snow, the back knee angling toward the front knee;
  - ankles at maximum dorsiflexion (about 25–30°) pressing into the boot tongues;
  - hips extended relative to heel-side (about 20–40° flexion), pushed inside the turn;
  - trunk upright to slightly back-arched, so the body forms a long line from the edge;
  - front shoulder leads (upper body rotated about 10–20° toward the nose and turn direction);
  - hands forward and low toward the inside snow, lead hand reaching toward the nose;
  - head looking up the turn, never down at the board.
- A light upper-body anticipation (shoulders rotated about 10–20° toward the next turn) separates skilled riders from beginners, consistent with "skilled = more trunk/pelvis rotation".

### Gaps
- No measured inclination-vs-edge-angle ratio for snowboarders was found. The 0.7–0.8 split is an ESTIMATE.
- No IMU study with heel vs toe joint angles was retrievable. The Japanese reaction-force/IMU papers and Klous 2014 may hold numbers but were not readable here.
- Most carving studies use alpine or hard-boot riders. Freestyle soft-boot carves are likely lower-edge and more upright (ESTIMATE).

## 4. Speed check / heel-side skid

### Takeaway
A heel-side speed check is a quick skid with the board across the fall line on the heel edge. The rider sits lower with bent knees and keeps the upper body upright over the board without bending at the waist. The arms stay quiet and slightly forward or out for balance. Sources disagree on whether the arms go forward or by the sides.

### Cited Findings
- Keep the knees bent. Locked joints lead to mistakes and injuries — [REI How to Snowboard](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/how-to-snowboard).
- Heel-side sideslip: keep the upper body upright, without bending forward at the waist, which pulls the upper body out of line with the board — [Burton Learn to Snowboard](https://www.burton.com/blogs/the-burton-blog/learn-to-snowboard) (search summary).
- Arm positions vary. Some say arms by the sides aligned over the board, some say slightly out in front and behind for balance, and one cue is to hold the pant seams to stop counter-rotation — search summary of [REI](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/how-to-snowboard), [Snowboard Addiction alignment](https://snowboardaddiction.com/blogs/training-education/how-to-ride-in-alignment), [Burton](https://www.burton.com/blogs/the-burton-blog/learn-to-snowboard).
- Skidded turns and J-turns are used to slow down. Sliding on the heel edge "digs in your heel edge" — [NC State Turning and Speed Control](https://ncstate.pressbooks.pub/skiingandsnowboarding/chapter/turning/); [Backcountry](https://www.backcountry.com/explore/how-to-turn-on-a-snowboard).
- Skiing (as an analogy): the angle of attack rises with lateral skidding, so it separates carving from skidding — [ISBS angle-of-attack paper](https://commons.nmu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2938&context=isbs).

### Inferences
- **ESTIMATE, speed-check key pose** (blend weight driven by the analog speed-check input):
  - board yawed 45–90° from the travel direction (match the physics yaw);
  - heel edge angle about 15–30° (lower than a carve; the board skids rather than locks);
  - knees 60–80° flexion, ankles about 20–25° dorsiflexion, hips 60–80° flexion (a deep "chair");
  - trunk leans forward about 20–30° from vertical toward the toe side to counter the backward sit and keep the CoM over the heel edge, so the chest doesn't fall back;
  - both arms forward of the chest at roughly shoulder to waist height, elbows about 60–90°, lead arm toward the nose;
  - head stays facing the original travel direction while the hips and board pivot (head and chest counter-rotate against the board by about 20–40°).
- When the input is released, the rider rises back to the glide pose over about 0.2–0.4 s (ESTIMATE). A short "spray" impulse adds a pelvis drop of about 3–5 cm and a little arm overshoot.

### Gaps
- No measured joint angles for heel-side skids or speed checks were found.
- No source quantifies how far a rider "sits back" in a skid.

## 5. Pre-jump approach (in-run right before the kicker)

### Takeaway
On the last stretch of the in-run, riders hold a stacked, centred, relaxed stance (knees slightly bent, back straight, weight even, head up), then compress deeper just before the lip to load the pop. Sources say gaze goes to the end of the ramp and the landing. None says the rider looks at the lip itself. In the takeoff, the knees extend faster than the hips, and harder tricks show more upper-trunk twist with less hip and knee flexion.

### Cited Findings
- Stacked, relaxed stance: knees slightly bent, back straight, head facing the end of the ramp, weight even between both feet — [Ridestore: How to jump on a snowboard](https://www.ridestore.com/mag/how-to-jump-on-a-snowboard/); [Dope Snow](https://www.dopesnow.com/mag/how-to-jump-on-a-snowboard/).
- Before the lip, bend the knees deeper than usual to prepare to pop, "like compressing a spring". Extend the legs until almost straight as the front foot reaches the lip — [Red Bull jump tips](https://www.redbull.com/us-en/snowboard-jumps-trick-tips); [Science Insights](https://scienceinsights.org/how-to-jump-on-a-snowboard-and-land-clean/).
- Approach cautiously and balance or airtime suffers; rush the takeoff and control suffers — [Burton Tricks to Learn](https://www.burton.com/blogs/the-burton-blog/snowboard-tricks-to-learn); [REI Beginner Tricks](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/beginner-snowboarding-tricks.html).
- Gaze: eyes on the landing zone, look ahead to where you'll land — [REI Beginner Tricks](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/beginner-snowboarding-tricks.html); [Ridestore](https://www.ridestore.com/mag/how-to-jump-on-a-snowboard/).
- Big-air takeoff (3D motion capture): as trick difficulty rises, upper-trunk twist and shoulder horizontal abduction increase significantly, while hip, knee and elbow flexion decrease significantly. Average hip angles increased in both legs with difficulty for most subjects (wording in the snippets conflicts) — [MDPI Applied Sciences 15(12):6618, 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/15/12/6618).
- Halfpipe IMU (elite vs novice): after take-off, knee flexion decreases faster than hip flexion. Elites take off earlier, control the board better, and land with a lower flexion range of motion (stiffer pattern). Both groups land with knee flexion below 63° — [Take-off and landing techniques in snowboard halfpipe (PMC13618448)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13618448).

### Inferences
- **ESTIMATE, sequence for the last about 1.5 s:**
  1. Straight glide pose (knees about 30–35°).
  2. Automatic pre-jump carve on the takeoff edge from `TAKEOFF_EDGE` (heel for frontside, toe for backside, flat base for straight and flip), using the carve key poses from section 3 at low edge (about 10–25°).
  3. Wind-up: shoulders pre-rotate opposite the spin direction by about 20–40° while the hips stay with the board. This matches "upper-trunk twist increases with difficulty" and should scale with the spin charge.
  4. Compress: knees to about 60–75°, hips about 60–70°, arms drawn in and down. This runs over the last about 0.3–0.5 s and scales with the charge.
  5. Pop: knee extension leads hip extension. This is the ground-side limit; air is covered elsewhere.
- Head: chin up, head level and stable, gaze moving from the lip to the knuckle or landing as the rider nears the takeoff (the "look at the lip" part is an ESTIMATE; sources only say landing or end of ramp).

### Gaps
- No numeric data on approach-phase angles from the big-air or halfpipe studies (full texts blocked).
- No source quantifies pre-wind-up shoulder angles in degrees.

## 6. "Life" motion while riding: micro-adjustments, arm sway, head stabilisation

### Takeaway
There is no snowboard-specific data. Locomotion research shows that humans partly stabilise the head in space, with small pitch and yaw velocities (about 17°/s peak in linear walking; under 90°/s in walking and running) and a head axis aimed at a stable point ahead. Instructors treat big arm swinging as a fault. Life motion should therefore be small: a stabilised head, gentle leg suspension against terrain, and subtle hand drift.

### Cited Findings
- During locomotion the head is stabilised in space "incompletely but adequately" so the VOR is not saturated. Median maximal head velocity in walking and running stays under 90°/s — [Pozzo et al., Exp Brain Res (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF00247595) (search summary).
- Peak head pitch and yaw angular velocities in linear locomotion are about 17°/s. The head axis aims at a fixation point about 1 m ahead in walking — [Mount Sinai: VOR during linear locomotion](https://scholars.mssm.edu/en/publications/the-human-vestibulo-ocular-reflex-during-linear-locomotion-2/); [PMC2768006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2768006).
- Horizontal canals stay within ±10° of earth-horizontal most of the time during walking (nose slightly down) — [PMC4986613](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4986613) (search summary).
- Swinging the arms or upper body to turn or shift is an inefficient flaw — [REI](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/how-to-snowboard).
- Elite halfpipe riders show less flexion range (stiffer) on landing than novices, so experts move less and more efficiently — [PMC13618448](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13618448).

### Inferences
- **ESTIMATE, procedural layers on the key poses:**
  - Leg suspension: the pelvis height follows a critically damped spring (f about 2–4 Hz, ζ about 0.7–1.0) driven by terrain and vertical board acceleration. Knee flexion modulates ±3–8° over bumps.
  - Head stabilisation: counter-rotate the neck so that head pitch and roll in world space change at no more than about 15–20°/s, and keep head roll within ±10° of horizon-level even while the body inclines in carves. This is the main thing that makes the rider read as "alive and skilled".
  - Arm sway: hands drift ±3–6 cm with a slow 0.3–0.6 Hz noise, plus inertial lag of about 0.1–0.15 s behind trunk accelerations. Avoid periodic swinging.
  - Weight micro-shifts: fore/aft CoM shift of ±1–2 cm and small ankle adjustments of ±2–3° at about 0.5–1 Hz on a straight glide.
  - Breathing: torso scale ±1 % at about 0.3 Hz (optional, only at higher quality levels).
- Skill read: less amplitude equals more expert (per the "stiffer elite" finding).

### Gaps
- No studies were found on head stabilisation or arm dynamics during snowboarding or skiing. All numbers above are transferred from walking and running research or are ESTIMATES.
