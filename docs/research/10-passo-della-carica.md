## Touch precision: how finely can a thumb drag be controlled on a phone, and how many levels fit in ~110 px (≈15–20 mm)?

### Takeaway
Absolute finger/thumb touch precision on phones is about **σ ≈ 1.5–1.6 mm** (one standard deviation of where touches land). Recommended minimum *tap targets* for one-handed thumb use are about **7.7–9.6 mm**. In a 15–20 mm drag range, a 1 % step is about 0.15–0.2 mm and a 5 % step about 0.75–1 mm. **Both steps are smaller than the finger's own noise**, so neither 100 nor 20 levels can be hit "blind" by motor control alone. Reproducible hits depend on watching live visual feedback while dragging, which is an inference from the numbers.

### Cited Findings
- FFitts law (Bi, Li, Zhai, CHI 2013) splits touch endpoint scatter into two parts. One is a relative-precision part under the speed–accuracy trade-off. The other is an **absolute finger precision σa** that does not depend on speed, so the classic Fitts ID log2(A/W+1) cannot capture the "fat finger" limit. — [FFitts Law, Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/ffitts-law-modeling-finger-touch-with-fitts-law/); [ResearchGate copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/262327000_FFitts_law_Modeling_finger_touch_with_Fitts%27_law)
- Measured σa values: a children's pointing study cites **Bi et al.'s σa ≈ 1.5 mm for adults (2D task)**. A follow-up smartphone study measured a calibration-task **σa = 1.59 mm**, almost identical to the σ observed in its main Fitts task (1.59 mm). — [Examining Fitts' and FFitts' Law for Children's Pointing (UF)](https://www.cise.ufl.edu/~brett.benda/files/FFittsLawForChildren.pdf); [Computing Touch-Point Ambiguity on Mobile Touchscreens (arXiv 2101.05244)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.05244v5) (values seen in search snippets only)
- There is no consensus on how to estimate σa, and calibration vs intercept methods gave inconsistent results. σa was smaller when users were told to "concentrate on accuracy". — [Ko et al., ChineseCHI 2021 (Stony Brook)](https://www3.cs.stonybrook.edu/~xiaojun/pdf/ChineseCHI2021.pdf); [arXiv 2101.05244](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.05244v5)
- Holz & Baudisch argue that touch error comes mostly from a **parallax/perceived-input-point problem**: users aim with features on top of the finger, while the device senses the contact area. With a generalized perceived-input-point model, the error offsets dropped to about **1.6 mm**. Their Ridgepad prototype was about **1.8× more accurate** than a simulated capacitive baseline, and optical tracking about 3.3×. — [Understanding Touch (academia.edu)](https://www.academia.edu/1186917/Understanding_Touch); [Ridgepad, HPI](https://hpi.de/baudisch/projects/ridgepad.html); [Generalized Perceived Input Point Model](https://christianholz.net/generalized_perceived_input_point_model)
- Parhi, Karlson & Bederson (MobileHCI 2006), one-handed thumb use: error rates showed no significant differences for targets **≥ 9.6 mm (discrete tasks)** and **≥ 7.7 mm (serial tasks)**. A secondary summary gives 9.2 mm and 9.6 mm instead, so the sources disagree. — [Microsoft Research page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=155150); [DOI 10.1145/1152215.1152260](https://dx.doi.org/10.1145/1152215.1152260)
- Thumb reach: Bergstrom-Lehtovirta & Oulasvirta (CHI 2014) model the thumb's **functional area** as a quadratic function of surface size, hand size and the index finger's position on the back of the device (n = 20). The drag zone should sit inside this area. — [KU record](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/modeling-the-functional-area-of-the-thumb-on-mobile-touchscreen-s); [DBLP](https://dblp.dagstuhl.de/rec/conf/chi/Bergstrom-LehtovirtaO14.html)
- Smartphone sliders: Colley, Mayer & Henze (CHI 2019) found that **slider orientation and visual style significantly affect the precision of entered values**. They also found systematic offset errors along the slider, which could be compensated. The numeric errors were not available from the snippets. — [ACM / Unpaywall](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F3290605.3300419); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332740979_Investigating_the_Effect_of_Orientation_and_Visual_Style_on_Touchscreen_Slider_Performance); [Lapland portal](https://research.ulapland.fi/en/publications/investigating-the-effect-of-orientation-and-visual-style-on-touch/)
- A patent notes that the ratio between slider length and finger contact area limits how precisely a value can be set on a touch slider. Another patent describes a mobile slider that **reduces the number of selectable values** as the finger nears the target, to make exact values easier to hit. — [US 9582184](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9582184); [US 10496265](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10496265)
- Continuous input suits many applications but is hard to use for precise control over a wide value range, so sliders often pair continuous movement with discrete steps. — [US 6433776](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6433776); Ramos & Balakrishnan's Zliding (UIST 2005) argues that the useful scale depends on the parameter and context. — [Zliding PDF](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~ravin/papers/uist2005_zliding.pdf)
- Unstabilized touch surfaces produce drag **jitter**, but stabilization strategies still allowed precise dragging (Sears & Shneiderman 1991). — [Sears 1991 PDF](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~ben/papers/Sears1991High.pdf)

### Inferences
- *(Inference)* Step sizes: with 110 CSS px ≈ 15–20 mm (the brief's figure; CSS px on phones is roughly 0.14–0.18 mm depending on the device), **1 % ≈ 0.15–0.2 mm** and **5 % ≈ 0.75–1.0 mm**. Both are below σa ≈ 1.5 mm. One finger-noise standard deviation therefore covers about **8–10 % of the range**.
- *(Inference)* If a player lifts or places the thumb "blind" and we count a level as reliably hit when about 95 % of attempts land in it (±2σ ≈ ±3 mm, so a 6 mm band), the realistic number of distinguishable levels is about **3 in a 15–20 mm range**. With tap-target guidance of 7.7–9.6 mm, it is only **2 levels**. This matches the common "25 % ticks" practice (4–5 anchor points).
- *(Inference)* While the thumb stays down and watches a live meter, closed-loop visual correction can do better than σa, because it removes re-acquisition and parallax error. Some precision is lost at release, since the contact point shifts as the finger lifts (lift-off drift; mechanism suggested by Holz & Baudisch's contact-area model, no measurement found). A realistic target for a skilled player with visual feedback is perhaps **±2–3 % repeatability**, a guess that is not sourced. That is enough to make 5 % bands meaningful but not 1 % steps.
- *(Inference)* Practical consequence: lengthening the drag range does more for precision than any quantization choice. 110 px is short compared with recommended target sizes. Options are a longer range (for example 35–40 mm) or a non-linear gain curve, finer in the zone where most good jumps live.

### Gaps
- Could not fetch the full texts of Colley et al. 2019 (slider error values), Holz & Baudisch's 95 %-button sizes, or Bi et al.'s exact σa per condition. Hosts were blocked, so the numbers above come from snippets.
- Found no study that measures **lift-off drift** or the minimum controllable increment of a *sustained thumb drag* on a phone, in mm.

## Game examples: continuous power with ticks, snapping, or discrete levels?

### Takeaway
Almost all power or charge meters in sports games are **continuous internally**, with **visual landmarks** (a target zone, rings, a "perfect" region, an overpower zone) rather than hard numeric steps. Discreteness tends to appear as **zones with distinct rules** (perfect, overpower, max-power penalty), not as uniform 5 % bands. Communities often add their own discrete grid on top of continuous inputs, such as Golf Clash "rings" and "+15 % / +35 %" notations, to make outcomes reproducible.

### Cited Findings
- **Golf Clash (mobile, drag-back):** "Perfect power" is reached when the ball touches all four points of the circle on the meter. Pulling past the circle enters **overpower**, where the screen vibrates and the aim needle moves wildly. Too much power is described as just as bad as too little. — [Playdemic FAQ: Adding Perfect Power](https://playdemic.helpshift.com/hc/en/6-golf-clash/faq/1287-golf-tip-adding-power); [Gamezebo guide](https://www.gamezebo.com/walkthroughs/golf-clash-tips-cheats-and-strategies/)
- Playdemic states that every shot uses the same app, rules and shot physics, which is a reproducibility claim. Curling the ball makes it "much harder to tell how far back you need to pull the ball to add perfect power". — [Playdemic FAQ 1136](https://playdemic.helpshift.com/hc/en/6-golf-clash/faq/1136-why-did-my-shot-land-where-it-did); [Playdemic FAQ 1285](https://playdemic.helpshift.com/hc/en/6-golf-clash/faq/1285-why-did-my-shot-not-land-where-i-expected-1613730444)
- Golf Clash community guides use **discrete notation** such as "Max +35 %", "Mid +15 %", "pull-back rings" and "CD OP" (circle-drop overpower). These are fan-made Google Slides, so they may be outdated, and the snippets did not explain the meaning of the percentages. — [Example community guide](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/112W2UYPWEqQBNKFv0ibJhwgFn4terlDRjBaBO_vm_9E/htmlpresent)
- **Mario Golf: Super Rush:** a vertical gauge with press-to-start and press-to-set power, so power is a continuous timing input. Hitting max power, the red top zone, risks a random left or right shift. Guides advise stopping just under the top, and say lower power gives more accuracy. Older Mario Golf games used a three-press meter that also set accuracy. — [Nintendo: Planning your shot](https://nintendo.com/us/whatsnew/planning-your-shot-in-mario-golf-super-rush); [Game8](https://game8.co/games/Mario-Golf-Super-Rush/archives/333996); [Screen Rant guide](https://screenrant.com/mario-golf-super-rush-beginners-guide-tips-strategies/)
- **Everybody's / Hot Shots Golf-style meters:** press to start the meter, press to set power (continuous), press a third time for accuracy. The Giant Bomb "power gauge" concept page describes a meter that swings, with power determined where the press stops it. — [GameRevolution review (Open Tee)](https://www.gamerevolution.com/review/33783-hot-shots-golf-open-tee-review); [Giant Bomb: Power Gauge](https://www.giantbomb.com/power-gauge/3015-784/)
- **Angry Birds:** launch angle and power come from where the player releases a point behind the sling, a continuous 2D input. The original had no trajectory preview, and visible trajectory lines were added later. A teaching resource notes that it is hard to hit an exact angle such as 35° vs 40°. — [Wikipedia (Angry Birds)](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Jaakko_Iisalo); [Science Buddies](https://www.sciencebuddies.org/blog/slingshot-science-the-physics-in-angry-birds)
- **Shredders (snowboard):** spins and flips are charge-based, and "the longer you hold a charge, the bigger the spin or flip". Tricks need charging spin and jump together, then releasing with precise timing. Reviewers call it hard to master, and one said tricks that had worked a hundred times suddenly failed. — [Windows Central review](https://windowscentral.com/shredders-xbox-review); [Kotaku review](https://kotaku.com/shredders-review-xbox-series-snowboarding-foam-punch-ea-1848674340)
- **SSX (2012):** the jump charge-up was kept but moved to the right stick. A preview found it "much less precise than previous games". In SSX 3, spins are pre-wound by holding jump plus a direction and releasing in the air. — [God is a Geek review](https://godisageek.com/2012/02/ssx-review/); [GameSpot preview](https://gamespot.com/articles/ssx-updated-hands-on-preview-mind-the-gap/1100-6337551/); [SSX 3 speedrun guide](https://www.speedrun.com/ssx3/guides/e6zze)
- **Session: Skate Sim:** a quarter-turn of the right stick gives a shove-it and a half-turn gives a 360 shove-it. These are **discrete outcomes from continuous stick rotation**. Options can make board spin speed depend on how hard the sticks are hit, which is analog. — [Kotaku: Session tips](https://kotaku.com/how-to-make-the-most-of-your-two-hours-with-session-1843998720); [TechRaptor](https://techraptor.net/gaming/opinions/how-session-redefines-skateboarding-games)
- **Skate (EA):** "Flick-It". The right stick does flips, and flicking it "just right" yields the different tricks. — [EA: Get control](https://www.ea.com/games/skate/skate/news/get-control)
- **Tony Hawk's Pro Skater:** the balance meter for manuals and grinds is a continuous needle that the player corrects. — [Giant Bomb: Balance Indicator](https://giantbomb.com/wiki/Concepts/Balance_Indicator); [StrategyWiki THPS2](https://strategywiki.org/wiki/Tony_Hawk%27s_Pro_Skater_2/Gameplay)
- **Grand Mountain Adventure (mobile):** spins come from dragging or holding a ski pole outward in the air, and flips from dragging up or down. Sources disagree on drag vs hold. — [Gamezebo guide](https://www.gamezebo.com/walkthroughs/grand-mountain-adventure-beginners-guide-tips-cheats-and-strategies/); [Pocket Gamer tips](https://www.pocketgamer.com/grand-mountain-adventure/grand-mountain-adventure-tips-for-this-beautiful-s)

### Inferences
- *(Inference)* None of the games found uses uniform hard 5 % bands for power. The prevailing pattern is **continuous value + a few landmark zones + feedback when crossing them** (Golf Clash vibration on overpower, Mario Golf's red max zone).
- *(Inference)* Hold-to-charge snowboard games (Shredders, SSX) map hold time to spin size, but the output is the **discrete rotation the rider completes** (180/360/540…). The analog charge selects a discrete outcome, and reviewers complain when the mapping feels inconsistent (Windows Central's "worked a hundred times, then failed").
- *(Inference)* Golf Clash players invented discrete notations (rings, +15 %, +35 %). This suggests that when the input is continuous, players who want reproducibility **build their own quantization out of visual landmarks**. Giving them landmarks (ticks) directly is cheaper than forcing hard bands.

### Gaps
- No primary source (developer interview or postmortem) was found that explains *why* these games chose continuous vs stepped power.
- No information was found on Snowboard Party, Alto (no power charge), Steep, Riders Republic or Everybody's Golf internals. Searches returned nothing specific.

## Game-design writing: analog vs discrete, snapping, ticks/notches, readability, learnability

### Takeaway
The design literature treats **input sensitivity** as something to match to the task (Swink). It recommends **snapping or detents where predictability matters more than expressiveness**, and **tick marks plus discrete haptic pulses** to make steps readable. Haptics must be optional reinforcement, never the only cue.

### Cited Findings
- Swink, *Game Feel*: an input device has an inherent "sensitivity". At one end is a button with two states; at the other is a mouse with near-complete freedom on two axes. A precision-demanding game paired with a low-sensitivity controller becomes frustrating, and input and reaction sensitivity should be tuned together. Example: Geometry Wars' turn rate was matched to the rotation of the Xbox 360 stick. — [Game Feel: The Secret Ingredient (Gamasutra)](https://GamaSutra.com/design/game-feel-the-secret-ingredient); [Wikipedia: Game feel](https://Www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_feel); [Rutgers lecture on Swink](https://sakai.rutgers.edu/access/content/group/0a4e9ee5-ee55-4b15-b778-52a05786e504/lectures/04_gamefeel.pdf)
- Treat analog input as an expression of player intent, not a direct game value. Use response curves so small movements stay fine-grained and extremes change faster. — [Game Developer: Analog input processing](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/opinion-analog-input-processing-and-delivering-great-controls)
- Snapping: an axial deadzone "snaps analog input to the only four input vectors that are actually relevant" for 4-way tile movement. This is a case where quantization helps because only a few outcomes are meaningful. — [Game Developer: Doing thumbstick dead zones right](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/disciplines/doing-thumbstick-dead-zones-right)
- Against snapping when smoothness matters: interpolating aim, even at high rotation rates, "feels much better than snapping towards the input position". — [Game Developer: Dual-stick shooter controls](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/everything-i-learned-about-dual-stick-shooter-controls)
- Notches: a forum argument links games clamping input near cardinal directions to Nintendo's notched analog stick, because players often want exactly straight. This is anecdotal. — [roadrunnertwice blog](https://roadrunnertwice.dreamwidth.org/606790.html)
- Apple HIG, sliders: **tick marks** help people understand the scale and make it easier to locate specific values. Labelling only the min and max is often enough. Give live feedback as the value changes. — [Apple HIG: Sliders](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sliders.md)
- Apple HIG, haptics: the **selection** haptic category is for "while the values of a UI element are changing" (`UISelectionFeedbackGenerator`). Built-in sliders and pickers already tick. Do not overuse haptics, or they lose meaning. — [Apple HIG: Playing haptics](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/playing-haptics.md); [UISelectionFeedbackGenerator](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/uiselectionfeedbackgenerator.md)
- Microsoft haptics guidance: use a firm pulse when stepping through discrete values. Haptics should reinforce clarity, and the experience must remain clear when haptics are reduced or off. — [Microsoft Design: Haptics design and implementation](https://microsoft.design/articles/haptics-design-and-implementation/)
- **Web constraint (relevant to this browser game):** `navigator.vibrate()` is **not supported on iOS Safari**. WebKit never shipped it and formally opposes it, and caniuse lists it as unsupported. Workarounds such as a hidden switch to trigger the Taptic Engine only fire on user-initiated click events (Safari 17.5+). One unverified GitHub issue claims recent support. — [W3C Vibration implementation report](https://w3c.github.io/vibration/reports/implementation.html); [caniuse](https://caniuse.com/mdn-api_navigator_vibrate); [web-features explorer](https://web-platform-dx.github.io/web-features-explorer/features/vibration); [MDN BCD issue (unverified claim)](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/issues/29166); [web-haptics browser support](https://www.mintlify.com/lochie/web-haptics/advanced/browser-support)

### Inferences
- *(Inference)* For this game, haptic ticks will work on Android Chrome but **not reliably on iPhone**, because a drag is not a click event. Visual ticks plus an optional audio "tick" must carry the readability on their own, which matches Microsoft's "clear without haptics" rule.
- *(Inference)* Swink's matching principle applied here: a thumb drag of 15–20 mm with σ ≈ 1.5 mm is a medium-sensitivity input. Mapping it to 100 distinguishable outcomes over-promises precision. Mapping it to a few clearly readable landmarks with smooth interpolation between them matches what the device can deliver.

### Gaps
- No GDC Vault talk or Tynan Sylvester (*Designing Games*) passage was retrieved that addresses charge-meter granularity directly. Sylvester's ideas on "elegance" and readable mechanics could be relevant, but no source was fetched.

## Recommendation: continuous mapping + visual/haptic ticks vs hard 5 % bands, and effect on reproducibility

### Takeaway
Recommended *(inference, built from the findings above)*:

1. **Keep the mapping from power to S-turn width/length continuous.** The physics is deterministic, so the same float input always gives the same S.
2. **Make it readable** with visual ticks every 25 % (optionally 10 %), live numeric or arc feedback, and a sound tick. Add a haptic tick where `navigator.vibrate` exists.
3. If designers want "notches", add **soft magnetism or hysteresis at the landmarks** rather than hard 5 % bands.

Hard 5 % bands do not improve reproducibility. The variance comes from the thumb (σ ≈ 1.5 mm ≈ 8–10 % of range), not from the mapping. Bands also add cliff edges: near a boundary, a sub-millimetre wobble flips the S by a full step.

### Cited Findings
- Finger noise of σ ≈ 1.5–1.6 mm is the binding limit, so input variance dominates. — [UF FFitts children study](https://www.cise.ufl.edu/~brett.benda/files/FFittsLawForChildren.pdf); [arXiv 2101.05244](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.05244v5)
- Reducing the number of selectable values near the target is a known technique for precise touch sliders, and it amounts to adaptive snapping. — [US 10496265](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10496265)
- Ticks aid locating values, and live feedback is recommended. — [Apple HIG: Sliders](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sliders.md)
- Snapping is good when only a few outcomes are meaningful, and smooth interpolation feels better when the outcome is a continuous motion. — [Game Developer: dead zones](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/disciplines/doing-thumbstick-dead-zones-right); [Game Developer: dual-stick controls](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/everything-i-learned-about-dual-stick-shooter-controls)
- Real games use continuous meters with landmark zones (Golf Clash perfect and overpower, Mario Golf's max-power zone), not uniform bands. — [Playdemic FAQ](https://playdemic.helpshift.com/hc/en/6-golf-clash/faq/1287-golf-tip-adding-power); [Nintendo](https://nintendo.com/us/whatsnew/planning-your-shot-in-mario-golf-super-rush)

### Inferences
- *(Inference)* **Reproducibility, "same input → same S":** both options are deterministic for identical inputs. What differs is whether *nearly identical* inputs give the same S.
  - Bands give the same S for most near-identical inputs, but a completely different S when two inputs straddle an edge. With a band about 1 mm wide and thumb σ about 1.5 mm, edge crossings are frequent, roughly 50 %+ of attempts aimed at a band centre would leave the band (rough estimate, assuming a Gaussian with σ ≈ 1.5× band width).
  - Continuous mapping gives a *proportionally* different S. Small error leads to a small change in the jump, which feels fair and learnable.
- *(Inference)* **Learnability:** the outcome is an S-turn geometry that ends in a kicker approach, which is a continuous physical result (line and speed). Continuous mapping lets players learn a smooth "more drag = wider S" model, in keeping with Swink's input–reaction matching. Bands add a hidden staircase the player can't feel at 1 mm resolution.
- *(Inference)* **Readability:** 20 bands is too many to read at a glance. Show 4–5 landmarks (0/25/50/75/100 %, or named zones linked to rotations such as 360/540/720…). If certain power values correspond to "correct" rotations, highlight those as **sweet-spot zones**, as with Golf Clash "perfect power", instead of quantizing everything.
- *(Inference)* **Skill expression:** continuous input keeps a skill ceiling (fine trimming for experts). Landmark ticks give beginners anchors. Hard bands cap expression at 20 values and make 1–4 % of extra thumb motion meaningless, but that is moot given motor noise.
- *(Inference)* **Recommended implementation sketch:**
  - Map `power = clamp(dx / range)` as a float and use it directly for S width/length. Optionally apply a gentle response curve that gives finer resolution in the commonly used mid range.
  - Add visual ticks at 25 %.
  - Add a short audio and haptic tick (Android `navigator.vibrate(5–10)`; none on iOS) when crossing a tick.
  - Optionally add a **soft detent**: within ±1.5 % of a tick, pull the value toward it, with hysteresis so jitter doesn't chatter.
  - Display an integer % in the UI but keep the float in physics, or quantize to 1 % only for display.
  - If the team wants exact replays or ghost reproducibility, quantize the *stored* value to 1 % (or 0.5 %). That is still effectively continuous, and it makes replays and tests exact without adding gameplay cliffs.
- *(Inference)* **When 5 % bands *would* be better:** if each band maps to a qualitatively distinct, nameable outcome (for example 20 named S presets shown as labels), if the game is meant to be menu-like or casual, or if networked or async competition requires players to share exact "recipes" like Golf Clash's "+15 %". Even then, fewer bands (4–8) fit the touch precision better than 20.
- *(Inference)* Timing matters too. The player must *start* charging at the right moment, so timing error (tens of ms × speed) probably adds more variance to the S placement than power granularity does. Testing should measure both.

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found comparing player performance or satisfaction for continuous vs stepped power meters in games. The recommendation rests on HCI precision data, platform guidelines and observed game patterns.
- No measured data was found for the repeatability of a sustained thumb drag with visual feedback, or for lift-off drift. A quick in-game playtest is advised: log the release `power` for repeated attempts at the same target and compute the SD in % of range.
