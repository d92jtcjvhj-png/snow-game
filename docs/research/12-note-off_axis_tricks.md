## Q1. Geometry of the rotation: axis tilt, inversions, degree counting, axis set at takeoff, edge

### Takeoff
A cork is one rotation about an axis that sits between vertical (spin) and horizontal (flip). Its name counts total degrees of rotation (1080 = 3 × 360) plus the number of off-axis "dips"/inversions (double = 2). I found no source that gives a measured tilt in degrees for snowboard corks. The tilt is set at the lip by dropping the lead shoulder, combined with the flat-spin edge (FS → heel, BS → toe). Rodeo, misty and underflip are "more flipped" relatives. Their axes are closer to horizontal, and they differ by whether the flip component is backward (rodeo, underflip) or forward (misty).

**Axis set at takeoff**
- BS cork: "The more you combine a downwards motion with your lead shoulder into the initiation of your spin, the more off axis you will be". Prerequisites are BS 540 plus wildcats/inverts. — [Snowboard Addiction BS cork 540](https://snowboardaddiction.com/blogs/jumping/how-to-backside-cork-540) (snippet)
- Spin rate and axis are set at takeoff, and angular momentum cannot change in the air. Arms in = faster, arms out = slower. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/olympic-big-air-snowboarders-use-physics-to-their-advantage/) (snippet)
- The takeoff phase generates most of the angular momentum. Takeoff errors "often result in unsuccessful landings or incomplete rotations". In BS 720→1800 on airbag (11 Chinese national-team riders), takeoff velocity, joint extension and angular momentum all rose with difficulty. — [Jiang et al. 2025, Applied Sciences 15(12):6618](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/15/12/6618) (abstract snippets; per-trick numbers NOT retrieved)
- Airborne tilt (twisting somersaults, Yeadon): moving one arm through 180° produces about 8° of tilt without ski equipment, but only about 4° with skis and boots, because the moment of inertia about the tilt axis is larger. In a freestyle-ski triple somersault, lowering one arm gave a 4° tilt that started the twist. Five twists needed three phases of asymmetrical arm movement. A larger tilt angle gives a larger twist component and a faster twist. — [Yeadon papers, Loughborough](https://www.lboro.ac.uk/microsites/ssehs/biomechanics/papers/twistflight97.pdf); [core.ac.uk Yeadon](https://core.ac.uk/download/288388971.pdf); [Yeadon 1993 III](https://dspace.lboro.ac.uk/dspace-jspui/bitstream/2134/5321/3/Yeadon1993c.pdf) (snippets)

**Edge (summary; details and conflicts in docs/research/02)**
- FS cork: heel-edge pop + lean back. BS cork: toe-edge pop + lead shoulder down. FS misty: toe edge only (FIS). BS rodeo: heel edge (Dopesnow/forum) vs toe-pressure accounts (conflict). Underflip: forum says the opposite edge to the BS rodeo. — see [docs/research/02](file:///home/user/snow-game/docs/research/02-offaxis-flip-straight.md) and the [FIS list](https://www.fis-ski.com/DB/snowboard/park-and-pipe/tricks-list.html)
- Switch versions: "cab" = switch frontside rotation. Switch backside is named literally ("switch backside 1980"). — [Whitelines cab 540](https://whitelines.com/snowboarding-advice/trick-tips/kicker/school-spin-frontside-rowan-coultas.html/3) (via 02); [Bleacher Report, Kimura 2026](https://bleacherreport.com/articles/25391747-kira-kimura-wins-gold-medal-snowboarding-big-air-olympics-2026)

### Inferences
- DERIVATION (rigid-body geometry, not sourced). Suppose the rider leaves the lip upright and rotates rigidly about an axis tilted θ from vertical. The body's long axis then sweeps a cone of half-angle θ around that axis, so its largest angle from vertical is 2θ, reached after half a revolution. The head goes below horizontal only if 2θ > 90°, so θ > 45°. Full head-down needs θ = 90° (a pure flip). This is consistent with "tilted but not fully inverted" (WSF) and with "feet above head" (Burton, in 02): corks plausibly live at θ ≈ 45–70°, rodeos and misties are closer to horizontal, wildcats and backflips sit at θ = 90° with no spin.
- DERIVATION: with a constant tilt, every revolution would produce one inversion, so N revolutions would give N inversions. Real double cork 1080s (3 revolutions, 2 inversions), triple 1440s (4 revolutions, 3 dips) and quad 1800s (5 revolutions, 4 flips) all have inversions = revolutions − 1. So the axis is not constant. Riders spend about one revolution near-flat, at the start (the setup/"wind" phase) and/or at the end (levelling out), and that rotation is credited as spin. McMorris's "dips" wording supports this picture of a varying axis. Game model: a trick = total yaw degrees D (multiple of 180) plus an inversion count k with k ≤ floor(D/360). Typical big-air pairs are k = D/360 − 1 (1080/2, 1440/3, 1800/4). The 2026 1980s are 5.5 revolutions; their inversion count was not found (probably quad or quint; unsourced).
- DERIVATION: NBC's "2 flips + 1 twist = 1080" is a mnemonic. Physically, the rider does 3 rotations about one moving tilted axis. Degrees are counted as yaw/spin as seen by judges, not as a vector sum.
- Yeadon's tilt numbers (4–8° per arm sweep) are far smaller than cork tilts. The cork tilt must therefore come mainly from the takeoff (shoulder drop and edge pop setting the angular-momentum vector), with arms and grab only fine-tuning in the air. Implementation: set the angular-momentum direction at the pop, and give the in-air input only a small authority (±5–10°) over the body's tilt about it.
- Which family a trick belongs to depends on the pitch direction relative to the spin. Backward pitch + FS spin gives a FS cork/rodeo (heel). Backward/sideways roll + BS spin gives a BS cork (toe). Forward pitch + FS spin gives a misty (toe). Backflip + FS rotation with the board passing under gives an underflip.

## Q2. Levelling out the axis, timing of opening, airtime needed per trick

### Takeoff
The sources available are coaching tips and snippets. Riders aim to finish each cork early (first cork by about 540–630° in a double), spot the landing about 270° before touchdown, then open (extend the body) to slow the spin and bring the axis upright. Grab timing changes the final degrees. No sourced airtime per trick was found apart from one ~2.5 s figure for a 2160.

### Cited Findings
- Double cork progression: "try to finish the first cork by 630° and see if you can even do it 540°"; watch head direction; push off with "chest up and looking up". Progression: cork to back → DC 1080 into foam → trampoline → water ramp → snow. — [Newschoolers, How double cork part 1](https://newschoolers.com/news/read/How-Double-cork-Part-1-double-cork-trampoline) (snippet; freeski source)
- Grab timing: grab at about 360°; grabbing as late as 540° "will make you rather double cork 1260 instead of 1080" (over-rotation). — same thread, summarised in search ([Newschoolers](https://newschoolers.com/news/read/How-Double-cork-Part-1-double-cork-trampoline)) (snippet, attribution moderately certain)
- Spotting: start looking for the landing at about 270° of rotation. Uncoil and extend the legs slightly as the landing approaches. Opening too early means "you won't come out of your cork". — [Ora cork 360 guide](https://ora.it.com/snowboard-tricks/how-to-cork-a-360-snowboard-trick); [Newschoolers forum](https://www.newschoolers.com/forum/thread/284530/How-do-i-land-corks-sut-am-i-doing-wrong-) (snippets; low-authority sources)
- Landing BS corks: keep both hands near the toe edge to dig in and stop the rotation. — [Snowboard Addiction BS cork 540](https://snowboardaddiction.com/blogs/jumping/how-to-backside-cork-540) (snippet)
- Tucking speeds rotation, extending slows it (conservation of angular momentum). — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/olympic-big-air-snowboarders-use-physics-to-their-advantage/); [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/61854-physics-olympics-winter-tricks-spin.html) (snippets)
- Removing tilt: in twisting somersaults the twist is stopped by asymmetrical arm movement in the opposite direction, and multi-twist skills need several phases of arm asymmetry. — [Yeadon, Loughborough](https://www.lboro.ac.uk/microsites/ssehs/biomechanics/papers/twistphysics00.pdf); [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/288388971.pdf) (snippets; the "three phases for five twists" detail is from the freestyle-ski triple-somersault study)
- Airtime: a 2160 (six full rotations) was described as falling from about 65 ft over about 2.5 s. — [Defector, How big can big air go](https://defector.com/how-big-can-big-air-go) (snippet; context unclear, possibly freeski). Max Parrot: landing triples "is all about if the jump is big enough". — [Sports Illustrated](https://www.si.com/edge/2015/03/20/max-parrot-technical-snowboarding) (snippet)
- Higher difficulty BS tricks (720→1800) needed higher takeoff velocity and higher angular momentum. — [Jiang et al. 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/15/12/6618) (abstract)

### Inferences
- ESTIMATE (unsourced, for tuning only). If a big-air rider gets about 2.5–3 s of airtime and needs about 0.4–0.5 s for setup and landing preparation, the average yaw rate is roughly: DC 1080 ≈ 3 rev / 2.2 s ≈ 1.4 rev/s; triple 1440 ≈ 4 / 2.2 ≈ 1.8 rev/s; quad 1800 ≈ 5 / 2.3 ≈ 2.2 rev/s. On a smaller kicker (about 1.5–2 s of air), DC 1080 needs about 2 rev/s. Treat these as starting values to tune against the jump in src/world/bigair.ts.
- Game mechanic suggested by the sources: (1) the pop sets ω and tilt; (2) the grab/tuck lowers the moment of inertia and raises ω (a late grab over-rotates); (3) "opening" raises the moment of inertia and lowers ω, and also rotates the body upright about the fixed angular-momentum vector (a small tilt correction); (4) the spot window starts about 270° before the target heading.
- The landing check should test three things at touchdown: body-up angle (inversion residual), board yaw versus travel direction, and remaining ω. Corks must reach near-zero tilt at touchdown. Rodeos and misties end upright by construction once their final half-revolution completes.

## Q3. Common failure modes for off-axis tricks and how they become falls

### Takeoff
Most off-axis falls come from wrong angular momentum at takeoff (under- or over-rotation), opening too early (stuck in the cork, landing on the back/side), or a heel-edge landing that keeps rotating. The dominant injury pattern in snowboarding is a backward fall onto the occiput or wrists, often after catching the back edge. Inverted-trick-specific fall data was not found.

### Cited Findings
- Takeoff errors "often result in unsuccessful landings or incomplete rotations". — [Jiang et al. 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/15/12/6618) (abstract)
- Opening too early: "you won't come out of your cork". — [Newschoolers forum](https://www.newschoolers.com/forum/thread/284530/How-do-i-land-corks-sut-am-i-doing-wrong-) (snippet)
- Heel-edge landing: "landing on the heel edge of your board can cause you to keep rotating after you've landed". The fix is hands near the toe edge. — [Snowboard Addiction BS cork 540](https://snowboardaddiction.com/blogs/jumping/how-to-backside-cork-540) (snippet)
- Late grab leads to over-rotation (DC 1080 becomes 1260). — [Newschoolers](https://newschoolers.com/news/read/How-Double-cork-Part-1-double-cork-trampoline) (snippet)
- Head injuries mostly come from backward falls with occipital impact, usually caused by the back (heel) edge catching. Wrist injuries are about twice as likely in backward falls. — [UBC FIB book, skull fractures in snowboarding](https://wiki.ubc.ca/Documentation:FIB_book/Prevention_of_Skull_Fractures_in_Snowboarding); [UBC FIB book, arm injuries](https://wiki.ubc.ca/Documentation:FIB_book/Kinetics_of_Arm_Injuries_in_Snowboarding_Falls) (snippets)
- World Cup big air injury rate was 2.3 per 1000 runs vs 1.3 overall (small sample of 10 big-air injuries). — [Thieme, snowboard injury survey](https://thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/html/10.1055/s-0041-1731702) (snippet)
- Real example: the top qualifier at the 2026 Olympics, Hiroto Ogiwara, fell on all three final runs, including a 2160 attempt. — [NBC Olympics/AOL summary](https://www.nbcolympics.com/news/kira-kimura-grabs-japan-first-snowboarding-gold-2026-winter-olympics-big-air) (snippet)

### Inferences
- Fall taxonomy for the game (inference, mapped from the above):
  1. Under-rotation (ω too low or a pop late off the lip): lands short of heading. Below about 90° short → lands on the tail/back seat or sideways and catches an edge → backward fall. Short by more than about 90° on a cork → lands on back/shoulder.
  2. Residual inversion/tilt (stuck in cork, opened too early or too late): touchdown with body tilt > ~30–45° → side/back slam.
  3. Over-rotation (late grab, too much pop): heading past target. Small → wash out/spin out on the heel edge. Large → lands switch-sideways.
  4. Heel-edge contact with remaining ω: keeps rotating → washout/backward fall (head/wrist risk). Toe-edge contact damps ω.
  5. Takeoff edge catch/no pop: wrong edge at the lip → axis random → treat as a bail.
- Thresholds are ESTIMATES to tune. Consistent with the 02/08 notes, the judging tolerance for heading is probably about ±45° clean, ±90° sketchy.

- No video-analysis study of big-air fall mechanisms by trick type (only SBX and general snowboarding).
- No sourced numeric tolerances for landing heading or tilt.

## Q4. Which off-axis tricks are standard, advanced, elite today (big air/slopestyle)

### Takeoff
Men's elite big air in 2025–26 is about 1980–2340 (switch BS 1980 won Olympic gold in 2026, and a 2340 was landed at X Games 2025). Triple corks (1440/1620) are the backbone of winning runs. Double corks (1080/1260) are advanced/standard for World Cup men. For women, triple 1440s (FS and BS) won Olympic gold in 2026, and the double cork 1080 is the entry level for finals. Single corks 540/720, rodeos, misties, wildcats and backflips are progression and amateur level.
